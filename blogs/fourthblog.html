Beyond the Prompt Box: How to Build an AI Productivity Stack That Actually Works

3 October 2026 · Bhavya's Day

For a long time, I thought entrepreneurship was something you became by starting a company. There was a familiar picture attached to it: a pitch deck, a registered entity, a few people around a table, and a willingness to take on a level of risk that most careers simply do not require.

But the more I think about it, the less useful that definition feels.

Entrepreneurship, at its core, is about agency. It is noticing that something could work better and deciding to do something about it. You might be building a company, leading a team, improving an internal process, working on a side project, or trying to solve a problem in your own community. The setting changes, but the underlying mindset is surprisingly similar.

What makes that mindset much more powerful is systems thinking. Instead of looking at one task or one problem in isolation, you start asking how everything around it connects.

The Shift From Doing the Task to Creating Value

Most of us are trained to become good at execution. We are given a responsibility, learn the process, complete it well, and move on to the next thing.

There is nothing wrong with being good at execution. The interesting shift happens when you start looking beyond the task itself.

  • Instead of asking what you have been assigned, ask where value is being lost. Maybe a process exists because it always has, not because it is still the best way to solve the problem.
  • Look at resources differently. Useful resources are not limited to money. Data that nobody uses, unused capacity, discarded materials, overlooked skills, and disconnected people can all become valuable when the right connection is made.
  • Think in experiments rather than bets. You do not need to take enormous risks to think entrepreneurially. A small experiment can tell you a lot before you commit serious time or money.

That last point matters because enthusiasm alone is not enough. You can work incredibly hard and still be solving the wrong problem. This is where systems thinking changes the way you approach the work.

Why Hustle Alone Is Not Enough

It is tempting to think that difficult problems can be solved by simply putting in more effort. Sometimes they can. But in a complex system, pushing harder on one part can create a completely different problem somewhere else.

A simple way of looking at a problem is:

Problem → Solution → Outcome

Real life is rarely that neat. One decision can change several other parts of the system, sometimes immediately and sometimes months later. A cheaper material might lower costs today but increase maintenance later. A faster process might improve output while quietly exhausting the people running it.

Systems thinking asks you to stay curious about those connections.

1. Look for the Second Effect

The first effect of a decision is usually easy to see. The second effect is where things get interesting.

Imagine a business deciding to reduce costs by changing a supplier. The immediate result looks positive. But what happens next? Does quality change? Does delivery become less reliable? Does customer satisfaction fall? Does the team spend more time fixing issues?

An entrepreneurial thinker does not stop at the first result. They follow the chain.

2. Pay Attention to Feedback Loops

Some changes reinforce themselves. Others create resistance.

  • Reinforcing loops can create momentum. A better product can improve customer retention, which can create more revenue, which can give the business more room to improve the product.
  • Balancing loops slow things down. Market saturation, limited supplier capacity, team fatigue, or rising costs can push back against growth.

Once you start seeing these loops, you stop asking only, “How do I make this happen?” You also start asking, “What will keep this working once I am no longer pushing it every day?”

3. Find the Leverage Point

This is one of my favourite ideas in systems thinking.

Sometimes we spend enormous amounts of energy fighting the visible problem when the real opportunity sits somewhere else in the system. Donella Meadows wrote about leverage points as places where a relatively small change can produce a much larger shift in the behaviour of a system.

In everyday work, that might mean fixing the handoff instead of asking people to work faster. It might mean changing the information people receive rather than reminding them to be more careful. It might mean removing one unnecessary step instead of adding another layer of monitoring.

The question becomes simple: what is the one structural change that could make everything else easier?

Four Ways to Practice This Every Day

  1. Map the whole journey. Follow an idea, product, piece of information, or customer request from beginning to end. Notice who touches it, where it waits, and where information gets lost.
  2. Look at waste as a signal. Wasted time, unused information, excess material, repeated meetings, and abandoned ideas can all tell you something about how the system is designed.
  3. Run small experiments. When the system is complicated, a huge change can create more uncertainty than clarity. Try something small, reversible, and measurable first.
  4. Build for resilience. A system that is perfectly efficient under ideal conditions can become fragile when one thing changes. Leave some room. Create alternatives. Make the system capable of adapting.

The Part I Keep Coming Back To

You do not need to call yourself an entrepreneur to think like one.

You also do not need to start a company tomorrow. The mindset begins much earlier than that. It starts when you notice a problem and become curious about why it exists. It grows when you connect resources that were sitting separately. It becomes useful when you test an idea instead of only talking about it.

Systems thinking adds another layer. It reminds you that every intervention becomes part of a larger environment. The goal is not simply to fix something today. It is to understand what happens next.

Maybe that is the more useful definition of entrepreneurship: not a title, not a company registration, and not a particular career path, but the habit of seeing possibilities and taking responsibility for turning them into something better.

And once you start looking at the world that way, you begin to notice opportunities everywhere.
